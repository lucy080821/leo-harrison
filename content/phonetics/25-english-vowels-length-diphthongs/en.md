# English vowels: length, diphthongs and reduction

## "Short" and "long" vowels: a simplified description

The RP tradition splits monophthongs into **short** (`/ɪ e æ ʌ ɒ ʊ ə/`) and **long** (`/iː ɑː ɔː uː ɜː/`). But actual duration **varies a great deal with context**, so this is only a simplified description:

- Vowels get **shorter** before **fortis** (voiceless) consonants: *bat* < *bad*, *seat* < *seed*. This is **pre-fortis clipping**, especially clear in GA, where vowels are short before fortis consonants and long elsewhere.
- Vowels are **longer** in **stressed** syllables, at the **end of an utterance** and in **slow** speech.
- So many phoneticians describe the difference between *ship* `/ʃɪp/` and *sheep* `/ʃiːp/` as a difference in **both length and quality**.

In GA, it is common to classify vowels as **tense** (`i u eɪ oʊ ɑ ɔ`) and **lax** (`ɪ ʊ ɛ ə æ`) instead of short-long.

## Diphthongs

RP has 8 diphthongs: **closing** (ending in a high position, gliding towards `/ɪ/` or `/ʊ/`) and **centring** (gliding towards `/ə/`):

| Group | Diphthongs | Keywords |
|---|---|---|
| Closing, towards `/ɪ/` | `eɪ aɪ ɔɪ` | FACE, PRICE, CHOICE |
| Closing, towards `/ʊ/` | `əʊ aʊ` | GOAT, MOUTH |
| Centring, towards `/ə/` | `ɪə eə ʊə` | NEAR, SQUARE, CURE |

The centring diphthongs are **changing** in modern British English: SQUARE is often a long monophthong `/ɛː/`, and CURE is often `/ɔː/` (*sure* `/ʃɔː/`). GA has no centring diphthongs: NEAR, SQUARE, CURE are **vowel + /r/** (`ɪr ɛr ʊr`).

Important for perception: diphthongs are **one sound**, not two, with a continuous formant movement (see the spectrogram).

## Vowel reduction

In **unstressed** syllables, vowels often **reduce** to a central position, mainly:

- `/ə/` (schwa): *about* `/əˈbaʊt/`, *banana* `/bəˈnɑːnə/`.
- `/ɪ/` or `/i/`: *happy* `/ˈhæpi/`, *roses* `/ˈrəʊzɪz/` (some accents use `/ə/`).

This is **undershoot** (module 2) turned into a rule of the language. Reduction is a core feature of **English rhythm** (next lessons), and a reason why the **stress pattern** of a word cannot be ignored.

## Syllabic consonants

In unstressed syllables, after an alveolar stop or fricative, `/ə/` often disappears and `/n/`, `/l/` **become the syllable nucleus**: *button* `[ˈbʌtn̩]`, *bottle* `[ˈbɒtɫ̩]`, *rhythm*.
