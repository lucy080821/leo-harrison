# The speech chain and the branches of phonetics

## What is phonetics?

**Phonetics** is the study of the **sounds of speech**: how they are made, how they travel and how listeners perceive them. It treats speech first of all as a **physical and physiological event**, without yet asking what function the sounds have in grammar or meaning.

## The speech chain

A familiar way to picture this is the **speech chain**: a message travels from the speaker's brain to the listener's brain in several stages.

1. **Linguistic**: the speaker chooses words and structure, and the brain plans movements.
2. **Physiological (articulation)**: nerves control the lungs, larynx, tongue and lips.
3. **Physical (acoustic)**: the movements create sound waves in the air.
4. **Physiological (hearing)**: the ear turns sound waves into neural signals.
5. **Linguistic**: the listener's brain recognises sounds, words and meaning.

## Three main branches

| Branch | Question | Typical tools |
|---|---|---|
| **Articulatory** | How is the sound made? | observation, MRI, tongue ultrasound, electrodes |
| **Acoustic** | What does the sound signal look like? | microphones, spectrograms, Praat |
| **Auditory** | How do the ear and brain perceive it? | listening experiments, hearing thresholds |

Each branch has its own character but they are tightly connected. How a sound is made (articulation) determines the signal (acoustics), and that signal is what the ear receives (audition). This course follows all three in that order.

## Phonetics and phonology

**Phonology** studies the **system** of sounds in a language: which sounds distinguish meaning, which can stand next to each other, which rules change them. Phonetics supplies the **data** and the tools of description; phonology looks for the **system** behind them. We return to phonology in module 6.

## Segmental and suprasegmental

- **Segmental**: individual sounds such as `/p/`, `/æ/`.
- **Suprasegmental** (or prosodic): stress, rhythm, intonation, tone, that is, phenomena spanning more than one segment.

## What is phonetics for?

Language teaching and learning, speech therapy, speech technology (recognition, synthesis), forensic phonetics (voice identification), documenting little-studied languages, and studying how sounds change over time.

## Writing is not phonetics

Phonetics studies **sounds**, not **letters**. English spells *though, through, tough* very differently from how they sound `/ðəʊ/`, `/θruː/`, `/tʌf/`. That is why we use a dedicated transcription system (next lesson).
