# Connected speech

When you hear native speakers talk quickly, does the sentence sound **very different** from the separate words? That is **connected speech**. Sounds at the border between two words affect each other. Understanding it helps your **listening** enormously.

## 1. Linking

The final consonant of one word attaches to the starting vowel of the next.

| Spelling | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| pick it up | `/ˈpɪkɪtʌp/` ("pi-ki-tup") |
| turn it off | `/ˈtɜːnɪtɒf/` |

**Linking /r/** (British): *far away* `/ˌfɑːr əˈweɪ/`. The /r/ appears only when the next word starts with a vowel.

## 2. Inserted glides

When two vowels meet, the mouth often inserts a glide:

- After `/iː/`, `/aɪ/`, `/eɪ/` a `/j/` is often inserted: *see it* `/siː jɪt/`.
- After `/uː/`, `/əʊ/`, `/aʊ/` a `/w/` is often inserted: *go on* `/ɡəʊ wɒn/`, *do it* `/duː wɪt/`.

## 3. Elision

A sound is **dropped**, often `/t/` or `/d/` in the middle of a cluster:

- *next day* `/neks deɪ/`
- *old man* `/əʊl mæn/`
- *friendship* `/ˈfrenʃɪp/`

## 4. Assimilation

A sound **changes** to resemble its neighbour, for ease of speech:

- *good boy* → `/ɡʊb bɔɪ/` (`/d/` becomes `/b/`)
- *that person* → `/ðæp ˈpɜːsn/`

## 5. Coalescence

Two sounds **merge into a new one**:

- *did you* → `/ˈdɪdʒu/` (`/d/` + `/j/` become `/dʒ/`)
- *meet you* → `/ˈmiːtʃu/` (`/t/` + `/j/` become `/tʃ/`)

## What is it for?

Mainly for **understanding**. When speaking, you are **not required** to imitate all of this: speaking slowly and clearly, word by word, is perfectly fine. But once you know them, you won't panic when you hear "gonna", "wanna" or "dijoo".
