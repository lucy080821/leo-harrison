# Comparing Vietnamese and English

This lesson is for anyone who wants to understand **why** Vietnamese speakers make certain pronunciation errors, not just that they do. This is the approach of **contrastive analysis**.

## How the two sound systems differ

| Feature | Vietnamese | English |
|---|---|---|
| **Tones** | Yes (6 in the northern accent) | None; pitch is used for intonation |
| **Word stress** | Not a major role | Yes, and it changes meaning or word class |
| **Rhythm** | Usually described as *syllable-timed* | Usually described as *stress-timed* |
| **Syllable structure** | Simple: (initial consonant) + (medial glide) + vowel + (final consonant) + tone | Complex: up to 3 initial and 3 to 4 final consonants |
| **Consonant clusters** | None | Very many (*strengths, texts*) |
| **Final consonants** | About 6 (p, t, k, m, n, ng) plus glides | Almost all consonants |
| **Vowels** | About 11 monophthongs and 3 diphthongs | 12 monophthongs and 8 diphthongs (RP) |
| **`/θ ð/`** | Absent | Present |

## The contrastive analysis hypothesis

The hypothesis (*Contrastive Analysis Hypothesis*, Lado 1957) says: **where the first language and the target language differ, learners will struggle**. Applied to Vietnamese speakers, we *predict*:

- Difficulty with `/θ ð/` (absent in Vietnamese).
- Dropping or adding sounds in **clusters** and **final positions**.
- Difficulty hearing **vowel length** (`/ɪ/` versus `/iː/`).
- Using **tones** or the even beat of Vietnamese in English, which weakens **stress** and **weak forms**.

## But contrast is not everything

The hypothesis has **limits**: not every difference causes errors, and some errors come **not from the first language** but from the target language itself (developmental errors). That is why **error analysis** and the idea of **interlanguage** (Selinker 1972) were added: the temporary system that learners build between the two languages.

## What to prioritise? Mutual understanding

There are two views (Levis, 2005):

- The **nativeness principle**: the goal is to sound like a native speaker.
- The **intelligibility principle**: the goal is that **listeners understand you**.

Research on English as a lingua franca (Jenkins, 2000) proposes a **priority "core"** for mutual understanding:

| In the core (prioritise) | Outside the core (less important) |
|---|---|
| Most consonants | `/θ ð/` |
| Aspiration of `/p t k/` | Weak forms of function words |
| Consonant clusters at word start | Stress-timed rhythm |
| Vowel length | Word stress and detailed pitch movement |
| Nuclear stress on the focus of the sentence | |

This is still a **debated** topic, but it helps teachers **avoid spending effort on things that matter little for being understood**.
