# The acoustics of consonants and VOT

Consonants differ from vowels in having an **obstruction** that makes the signal change rapidly. Each group has its own acoustic "fingerprint".

## Plosives (stops)

A typical stop has these stages on a spectrogram:

1. **Closure**: silence (or a small bar at the bottom if voiced).
2. **Release burst**: a short, broad-spectrum smear.
3. **Aspiration**: a light friction before the vowel starts (if present).
4. **Formant transitions**: the formants of the following vowel move to their steady positions, carrying information about the consonant's **place of articulation**.

## VOT: voice onset time

**VOT** is the time between the **release** of a stop and the **onset of vocal fold vibration** for the vowel. It is the main tool for distinguishing voiced from voiceless consonants across languages.


![Voice onset time](/phonetics/vot.svg)
*Figure 3.7. Three kinds of VOT: voicing lead (negative VOT), short lag and long lag (aspirated).*

| Type | VOT | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Voicing lead** | **negative** (vibration before the release) | `/b d g/` in Spanish, French; sometimes in English |
| **Short lag** ("tenuis") | near **0** (about 0 to a few tens of ms) | `/b d g/` often in English; `/p t k/` in French |
| **Long lag** (aspirated) | **large positive** (about 60 ms and above) | word-initial `/p t k/` in English |

These values are only **illustrative**: for velar stops, short-lag VOT is typically 20 to 30 ms, light aspiration about 50 to 60 ms, moderate about 80 to 90 ms, and over 100 ms is strong aspiration. A commonly shown example measures about 95 ms for /t/ in *tie* and about 25 ms for /d/ in *die*. **VOT varies with speaker, position, speech rate and language**, so always compare under the same conditions (see Lisker and Abramson 1964; Cho and Ladefoged 1999; Abramson and Whalen 2017).

## Fricatives

The source is **noise** (see the source-filter lesson), so the spectrum is continuous. The shape of the noise spectrum depends on the constriction and the **cavity in front of it**.

- **Sibilants** `/s z ʃ ʒ/`: very strong noise concentrated at **high** frequencies; `/s/` has higher-frequency energy than `/ʃ/` because the front cavity is shorter.
- **Non-sibilants** `/f θ v ð/`: weaker, flatter noise.
- Voiced fricatives add **vertical striations** (voicing) over the noise.

## Nasals

When the velum lowers, the nasal cavity joins in. The spectrum shows:

- A **nasal murmur**: low-frequency energy concentrated below about 300 Hz.
- **Antiformants** (spectral "holes") created by the nasal cavity.
- Overall amplitude weaker than vowels, and the **transitions** of neighbouring vowels indicate the place of `/m n ŋ/`.

## Approximants

Because the obstruction is very light, they resemble vowels: clear formants. For example `/j/` resembles `[i]` (high F2) and `/w/` resembles `[u]` (low F2). English `/ɹ/` stands out with a **very low F3** (like `/ɝ/` in the previous lesson). `/l/` has a low F1 and differs in F2 between "clear l" and "dark l".

## Summary of cues

| Group | Main cues |
|---|---|
| Stops | closure gap, burst, VOT, formant transitions |
| Fricatives | continuous noise; sibilants strong at high frequencies |
| Nasals | low-frequency murmur, antiformants |
| Approximants | clear formants like vowels, low F3 for `/ɹ/` |
