# Allophonic variation in English consonants

This lesson is the **phonetic** side of an issue we will view from the phonological angle in module 6: one phoneme is pronounced **differently** in different positions.

## Fortis and lenis consonants

Pairs such as /p/-/b/, /t/-/d/, /s/-/z/ are often called **fortis** (p t k tʃ f θ s ʃ) and **lenis** (b d ɡ dʒ v ð z ʒ). This reflects that "voiced/voiceless" is **not** always the main difference in English:

- Initial lenis consonants are often **weakly voiced**: /b d ɡ/ often have a VOT near 0 or slightly negative, while fortis /p t k/ have **long VOT** (aspiration).
- Vowels are **shorter** before fortis consonants: *bat* has a shorter vowel than *bad* (especially clear in GA).

## Aspiration

The voiceless stops `/p t k/` and the affricate `/tʃ/` are **aspirated** at the start of a stressed syllable: *pin* `[pʰɪn]`, *tin* `[tʰɪn]`, *kin* `[kʰɪn]`. They are **not** aspirated after `/s/`: *spin* `[spɪn]`, *stop*, *skin*. They are also less aspirated when followed by a completely unstressed vowel.

## Glottalisation

At the end of a syllable, voiceless stops can be **reinforced or replaced** by a glottal stop `[ʔ]`. Common in British accents: *button* `[ˈbʌʔn̩]`. In GA, /t/ also becomes `[ʔ]` **before a syllabic /n/** or before a non-syllabic consonant: *button* `[ˈbʌʔn̩]`, *what* `[wʌʔ]`.

## Flapping in GA

In GA, `/t/` and `/d/` become a **flap** `[ɾ]` when they come **after a vowel or /r/** and **before an unstressed vowel** (or a syllabic consonant other than /n/): *later* `[ˈɫeɪɾɚ]`, *city*, *party*. As a result pairs such as *ladder* and *latter* sound **alike** (they merge). Between vowels before /n/, the flap can be nasalised, so *winter* sounds almost like *winner*. Flapping is blocked in *seduce*, *retail*.

## Clear l and dark l

- **RP**: **clear** `[l]` before a vowel in the same syllable (*leaf, silly*); **dark** `[ɫ]` before a consonant or word-finally (*full, milk*).
- **GA**: **dark** l almost everywhere.

## Realisations of /ɹ/

The phoneme `/ɹ/` has several realisations. In RP it is a **postalveolar approximant** `[ɹ̠]`. In GA it can be `[ɹ̠]` or `[ɻ]` (retroflex), and may have a "**bunched**" form where the tongue body is raised without lifting the tip. The two sound alike but are articulated differently: a nice example of **different articulations producing the same sound**.

## Yod-dropping

In GA, `/j/` is usually dropped after an alveolar consonant in a stressed syllable: *new* `/nuː/`, *Tuesday*, *duke*. RP keeps `/j/`: *new* `/njuː/`.

## Summary

| Phenomenon | RP | GA |
|---|---|---|
| Aspiration of /p t k/ in stressed syllable onset | yes | yes |
| Flapping of /t d/ between vowels | no (keeps [t]) | yes |
| Glottal stop at syllable end | common (British accents) | before /n̩/ |
| Dark l | before consonants, word-finally | almost everywhere |
| /ɹ/ after a vowel | not pronounced | pronounced |
| Yod-dropping after /t d n/ | no | yes (many words) |
