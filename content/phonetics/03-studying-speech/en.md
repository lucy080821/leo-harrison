# How speech is studied

## Two approaches

**1. Impressionistic analysis**: the phonetician listens and transcribes by ear, after long training. Fast and flexible, and the main method in fieldwork, but it depends on the listener.

**2. Instrumental analysis**: using instruments to obtain objective, repeatable numbers. The two are often combined.

## The main instrumental techniques

| Goal | Technique | Tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Acoustics | microphone, spectrogram, software such as **Praat** | frequency, formants, duration, intensity |
| Vocal folds | **EGG** (electroglottography) | vocal fold contact over time |
| Tongue contact | **EPG** (electropalatography) | where the tongue touches the palate |
| Tongue shape | tongue **ultrasound**, MRI | the shape of the tongue and mouth |
| Articulator movement | **EMA** (electromagnetic articulography) | trajectories of sensors on the tongue and lips |
| Airflow | airflow and pressure meters | nasal and oral airflow, aspiration |
| Perception | listening experiments: identification, discrimination | how listeners categorise sounds |

No technique is perfect: each measures one aspect and has limits (MRI is slow, ultrasound struggles to show the tongue tip).

## Corpora and fieldwork

- **Corpus**: a collection of recorded and annotated speech that allows the study of many speakers.
- **Fieldwork**: recording languages outside the lab, often needing only simple equipment and impressionistic plus acoustic analysis.

## Research ethics

When recording others: obtain **informed consent**, explain the purpose, allow withdrawal and protect identity and data. A voice is personal data.

You will use two things right on the page: a **vowel synthesiser** and a **live spectrogram** from your microphone or an audio file. They run entirely in your browser and no audio is sent anywhere. Module 3 introduces them, and the Praat lesson shows how to do the same with professional software.
