# The phonetics of Vietnamese tones

## What is a tone?

A **tone** is a variation of **pitch** (F0) on a **syllable** that **distinguishes word meaning**. Hanoi Vietnamese has **6 tones**: ngang, huyền, sắc, hỏi, ngã, nặng (some accents, like Saigon, have fewer because hỏi and ngã merge).

## Describing with Chao numerals

A familiar way to describe them is **Chao numerals** (proposed by Y. R. Chao): numbers from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest) record the **F0 contour** from the start to the end of the syllable. For example `33` is level at mid height, `35` is rising from mid to high, `21` is falling from low-mid to low.

| Tone | Commonly described features (Hanoi) | Approximate Chao numeral |
|---|---|---|
| **ngang** (ma) | level, mid | `33` |
| **huyền** (mà) | low, gently falling, sometimes breathy | `21` |
| **sắc** (má) | rising, mid to high | `35` |
| **hỏi** (mả) | falling-mid, often slightly dipping | `31` or `313` |
| **ngã** (mã) | rising, **creaky** (interrupted) | `35` to `45` |
| **nặng** (mạ) | low, **short**, ending in glottal closure | `32` or `21` |

The exact values **differ between authors, speakers and measurement methods**, so treat the table as a **general picture**. The tone names (ngang, huyền...) are spelling names, not acoustic descriptions.

## Pitch is not everything

Vietnamese tones are **multidimensional**: besides F0 there is:

- **Voice quality** (phonation): ngã often has **creaky voice** (laryngealisation), nặng has **glottal closure**, huyền sometimes has **breathy voice**.
- **Duration**: nặng is usually the **shortest**.
- **Intensity**: varies with tone.

In syllables ending in unreleased `p t k`, only the **sắc** and **nặng** tones occur.

## Tone and intonation

Vietnamese has **both tone and intonation**. Intonation (questions, emphasis, emotion) is **laid over** the tones: for example at the end of a question, the pitch of a sắc syllable rises higher while a huyền syllable still "falls" but from a higher level. Because each syllable is already "occupied" by its tone, **the range of pitch change available for intonation is limited**.

English has no tones, so pitch is **free** to express intonation over a whole phrase. Vietnamese learners speaking English may unconsciously apply **tones to each English syllable**, or **stress syllables evenly**, making intonation and rhythm sound different from native speakers. This is a common hypothesis that needs to be tested with measurements rather than a firm claim.
