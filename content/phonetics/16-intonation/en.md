# Intonation

**Intonation** is how the **pitch of the voice** rises and falls across a whole sentence. The same sentence with different intonation means something different.

## Unlike Vietnamese tones

In Vietnamese, pitch belongs to **each syllable** and changes the meaning of the word (*ma, mà, má*). In English, pitch **does not change the meaning of the word**; it tells us what the **whole sentence** means: question or statement, certain or hesitant, and where the focus is.

Vietnamese learners sometimes use word-level tones on English words by accident, which makes sentences sound "bumpy". Think of pitch over a **whole phrase**, not per word.

## Tone units and the nucleus

We divide speech into **tone units**, often separated by a small pause:

*When I arrived* | *the shop was closed.*

Each unit has a **nucleus**: the syllable where the pitch **starts to rise or fall**. It is usually in the **most important word** of the unit.

## Three basic tunes

| Tune | Mark | Often used for |
|---|---|---|
| **Fall** | ↘ | statements, Wh- questions, commands |
| **Rise** | ↗ | Yes/No questions, unfinished items in a list |
| **Fall-rise** | ↘↗ | hesitation, "but...", not finished yet |

Examples:

- *I live in Hanoi.* ↘ (statement)
- *Where do you live?* ↘ (Wh- question)
- *Do you live in Hanoi?* ↗ (Yes/No question)
- *It's good...* ↘↗ (implying "but...")

## Move the stress, change the meaning

Moving the nucleus to a different word shifts the focus:

- *I didn't say HE stole it.* (someone else said it)
- *I didn't SAY he stole it.* (I only implied it)
- *I didn't say he STOLE it.* (he did something else)

## An honest note

The tunes above are **tendencies**, not absolute rules. Regions and accents differ (for example many younger speakers rise at the end of statements too). Your goal is to **hear** these differences first, and then gradually use them.
