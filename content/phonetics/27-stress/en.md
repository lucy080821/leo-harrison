# English word stress

## What is stress?

**Stress** is the prominence of one **syllable** relative to the others in a word. Acoustically it combines **F0**, **duration**, **intensity** and **vowel quality** (see module 3). In transcription, `/ˈ/` goes before the syllable with **primary stress** and `/ˌ/` before the one with **secondary stress**.

## English stress is "free"

Unlike languages with fixed stress (French usually on the last syllable, Polish on the penultimate), English stress is **not fixed** but depends on the word. It is **contrastive**:

| Word class | Examples |
|---|---|
| Noun | *record* `/ˈrekɔːd/`, *present* `/ˈprezənt/`, *import* `/ˈɪmpɔːt/` |
| Verb | *record* `/rɪˈkɔːd/`, *present* `/prɪˈzent/`, *import* `/ɪmˈpɔːt/` |

## Tendencies of placement

Roach and Cruttenden both stress that there is no absolutely simple rule, but there are **tendencies** by structure:

- **Two-syllable** nouns and adjectives: usually stress the **first** (*TAble, HAPpy*).
- **Two-syllable** verbs: usually stress the **second** (*deCIDE, reLAX*).
- Words with **-tion, -sion, -ic, -ity**: stress falls on the syllable **just before** the ending (*eduCAtion, ecoNOmic, possiBIlity*).
- Words with **-ee, -eer, -ese**: stress falls on **the ending itself** (*employEE, engiNEER, JapaNESE*).
- **Compound nouns**: usually stress the **first** element (*BLACKbird* is a kind of bird, *black BIRD* is a bird that is black).

## Stress shift with suffixes

| Word | Transcription | Primary stress |
|---|---|---|
| photograph | `/ˈfəʊtəɡrɑːf/` | syllable 1 |
| photography | `/fəˈtɒɡrəfi/` | syllable 2 |
| photographic | `/ˌfəʊtəˈɡræfɪk/` | syllable 3 (secondary stress on 1) |

Note: when stress moves, the **vowel quality also changes** (unstressed vowels reduce to `/ə/`). So learning stress and learning vowels are **one task**.

## Stress in Vietnamese and the consequence

Vietnamese uses **tones** on each syllable and has no word stress in the English sense. Vietnamese learners therefore often **stress syllables evenly** or use pitch like a tone. For English listeners, wrong stress placement can make a **word hard to recognise** (see module 7).

## Rhythm: the stress-timing hypothesis

Pike (1945) and Abercrombie (1967) proposed that English rhythm is **stress-timed**: stressed syllables fall at **regular** time intervals, and unstressed syllables are squeezed to fit. Vietnamese is classed as **syllable-timed**: each syllable has a similar length.

However, **measurement** studies (for example Roach 1982 and later) show the regularity is **not absolute**: intervals between stresses vary with the number of syllables. Most researchers today treat this as a **perceptual tendency** and a difference in **syllable structure and vowel reduction** between languages, rather than an absolutely regular rule. Even so, the difference in **perceived rhythm** between English and Vietnamese is real.
