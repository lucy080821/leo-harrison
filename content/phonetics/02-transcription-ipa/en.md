# Transcription and the IPA chart

## Why a dedicated set of symbols?

The spelling of most languages does not record sounds consistently. Phoneticians need a system with **one symbol per sound** to describe any language. That is the **International Phonetic Alphabet** (IPA), maintained by the International Phonetic Association.

*Broad and narrow transcription of the word pin*

## The parts of the IPA chart

The official chart has seven blocks:

| Block | Content |
|---|---|
| Consonants (pulmonic) | consonants made with air from the lungs, arranged by place and manner |
| Consonants (non-pulmonic) | ejectives, implosives, clicks |
| Other symbols | co-articulated sounds, flaps, etc. |
| Vowels | the vowel chart arranged by height and backness |
| Diacritics | add detail to a symbol, e.g. aspiration, devoicing |
| Suprasegmentals | stress, length, breaks |
| Tones and word accents | symbols for tone languages |

The official chart is freely reusable under **CC BY-SA 3.0** (with attribution) on the International Phonetic Association's website. In this course the tables are rebuilt and explained lesson by lesson.

## Broad and narrow transcription

| Type | Brackets | Records | Example *pin* |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phonemic** (*broad*) | `/ /` | meaning-distinguishing units | `/pɪn/` |
| **Phonetic** (*narrow*) | `[ ]` | detail of the actual pronunciation | `[pʰɪn]` |

The more detail (narrow transcription), the more information but also the more effort and the more it depends on the listener. Dictionaries normally use broad transcription.

## Transcription is a theory, not a faithful copy

Remember: transcription is **not an objective copy** of the sound. Each way of writing carries an analytical choice, for example whether the "ch" of *chair* is one sound `/tʃ/` or two sounds `/t/ + /ʃ/`. Two phoneticians may write slightly differently and both be right from their own viewpoint.

## Some symbols to get used to

| Symbol | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `/ʃ/` | "sh" | *ship* `/ʃɪp/` |
| `/θ/` `/ð/` | voiceless and voiced "th" | *think* `/θɪŋk/`, *this* `/ðɪs/` |
| `/ŋ/` | final "ng" | *sing* `/sɪŋ/` |
| `/j/` | "y" | *yes* `/jes/` |
| `/ɡ/` | "g" | *go* `/ɡəʊ/` |
| `/ə/` | a very light "uh" (schwa) | *about* `/əˈbaʊt/` |
| `/ˈ/` `/ˌ/` | primary and secondary stress | *education* `/ˌedʒuˈkeɪʃn/` |
| `/ː/` | long sound | *see* `/siː/` |

An example of the two accents: *go* is `/ɡəʊ/` in standard British English (RP) and `/ɡoʊ/` in General American (GA). We compare the two systems in module 5.
