# Phonological rules and sound change

## Phonological rules

A **phonological rule** describes how a phoneme is **realised** in environments. Standard notation:

**A → B / C __ D**

meaning "A becomes B when it follows C and precedes D". The underline `__` marks the position of A. `#` is a word boundary; `∅` means "nothing".

## Examples in English

| Rule | Reads | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `/p t k/ → [pʰ tʰ kʰ] / # __ (stressed V)` | voiceless stops are aspirated at the start of a stressed syllable | *pin* `[pʰɪn]` |
| `/t d/ → [ɾ] / V __ V (unstressed)` (GA) | flapping | *later* `[ˈɫeɪɾɚ]` |
| `/n/ → [m] / __ [labial]` | place assimilation | *in+possible* → *impossible* |
| `/l/ → [ɫ] / V __ , __ #` | dark l | *full* `[fʊɫ]` |
| `/t/ → ∅ / [s] __ # C` | /t/ elision | *next day* `/neks deɪ/` |

## Foundation: "underlying" and "surface"

Rules link the **underlying form** (the phoneme) with the **surface form** (the phonetic). For example the plural suffix: underlying `/z/`, surface `[s]` after a voiceless sound. The same morpheme can have several **surface forms** (**allomorphs**).

## Common types of rules

- **Assimilation**: a sound becomes more like a neighbour (voicing, place, nasalisation).
- **Deletion**: a sound is lost.
- **Epenthesis**: a sound is inserted (*pl-* becomes *p-uh-l*).
- **Lenition**: a sound becomes "weaker" (stop to flap, fricative or approximant).
- **Metathesis**: two sounds swap places.

## Rule ordering

When several rules apply, **order** can matter. For example in GA, the rule **changing /t d/ into [ɾ]** can make *latter* and *ladder* sound alike, even though their underlying forms differ (`/t/` and `/d/`). Several underlying forms yielding the same surface form is **merger** or **neutralisation**.

## From phonetics to phonology to sound change

The relation between phonetics and phonology is **two-way**. A typical story:

1. **Phonetic**: a vowel is slightly nasalised before a nasal consonant (mechanical coarticulation).
2. **Phonologisation**: listeners reinterpret that influence as a **rule** of the language.
3. **Sound change**: the nasal consonant is lost and the **nasal vowel** remains, becoming a new phoneme (as in French *bon* `[bɔ̃]`).

So many historical sound changes originate in small **phonetic influences**, and that is why phonetics helps **explain** why sounds change in certain directions.

## Sound change over time

Historical linguists found that sound change is **regular** (a rule applies to all words meeting its conditions), an idea of the Neogrammarian school. A familiar English example: the vowel of *mine* was once close to `/iː/` and shifted to `/aɪ/` in the **Great Vowel Shift**.

## Why are some sounds more common?

Two ideas explain the distribution of sounds around the world (both topics of MIT 24.915):

- **Quantal theory** (Stevens): favours sounds in articulatory regions that are acoustically stable.
- **Dispersion theory** (Lindblom): vowel systems tend to **spread** across the formant space to stay distinguishable.

They show that the structure of sound systems is not arbitrary but shaped by **phonetics**.
