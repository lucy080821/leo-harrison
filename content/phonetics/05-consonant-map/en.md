# The consonant map

English (standard British) has **24 consonants**. That sounds like a lot, but three "keys" let you read all of them.

## Three keys

1. **Voiced or voiceless** (*voicing*): do the vocal folds vibrate?
2. **Place of articulation** (*place*): where in the mouth is the sound made?
3. **Manner of articulation** (*manner*): how is the airflow obstructed?

For example `/b/` is a **voiced bilabial plosive**. Naming it that way already describes exactly how it is made.

## Manner: how the air is obstructed

| Manner | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Plosive | Air fully blocked, then released | `/p b t d k ɡ/` |
| Fricative | Air squeezes through a narrow gap, making a hiss | `/f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/` |
| Affricate | A plosive followed right away by a fricative | `/tʃ dʒ/` |
| Nasal | Air escapes through the nose | `/m n ŋ/` |
| Lateral | Air passes along the sides of the tongue | `/l/` |
| Approximant | Very light obstruction, close to a vowel | `/r j w/` |

## "Twin" pairs

In the chart you can spot many pairs that differ only in voicing: `/p/`-`/b/`, `/t/`-`/d/`, `/k/`-`/ɡ/`, `/f/`-`/v/`, `/s/`-`/z/`. So when you learn one sound, you already know half of its pair.

## A small note

Some sounds have extra "detail" depending on where they sit in a word (for example `/p/` at the start of a word is released with more air). That belongs to module 5. For now, just remember the three keys.
