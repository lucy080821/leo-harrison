# Speech perception and its theories

## Perception is not reading a spectrogram

A real listener **understands** speech; they do not "read" a spectrogram. They must separate speech from noise, normalise across speakers, and recognise words in a continuous stream. **Speech perception** studies how that happens.

## Acoustic cues

A sound is usually recognised through **many cues** at once. For example the difference between word-initial /b/ and /p/ can be: **VOT**, the amount of aspiration, **F1 at vowel onset** and F0 at vowel onset. Cues can **trade off** against each other (*trading relations*): a slightly shorter VOT can be compensated by a different F1.

## Categorical perception

Researchers at **Haskins Laboratories** (Liberman, Delattre and Cooper, 1958) used **synthetic** speech varied in small, equal steps and found that listeners **hear distinct categories**. Lisker and Abramson (1970) ran the classic experiment on **VOT**: they synthesised a series of /Ca/ tokens with VOT ranging from negative to positive (37 values spanning from about 150 ms before to 150 ms after the release).

Two tests:

1. **Identification**: listeners label each sound. The result is a very steep **S-shaped** curve: an abrupt switch from "ba" to "pa" at a VOT **boundary**, not a gradual change.
2. **Discrimination**: listeners say whether two sounds are the same. They discriminate **almost perfectly** two sounds on opposite sides of the boundary, but **poorly** two on the same side (even when equally far apart in ms).

This is **categorical perception**. Three cautions:

- It is **clearer for consonants** than vowels.
- Later experiments showed that **non-speech** sounds can produce similar effects, so it is not certain that this is a **speech-specific** mechanism.
- Listeners can **discriminate** many differences within a category if asked to, so the "category" is not an absolute barrier.

## Lack of invariance

A phonetician would like a **fixed** acoustic cue for each sound. But there isn't one: the same /d/ has different formant transitions depending on the following vowel (coarticulation), and differs between speakers. This is the **lack of invariance** problem and one of the central questions of the field.

## The main theories (introduction)

| Theory | Main idea |
|---|---|
| **Motor theory** (Liberman) | we recognise sounds by "covertly" inferring the **articulatory gestures** that produced them |
| **Direct realism** (Fowler) | listeners directly perceive **articulatory gestures** from the signal |
| **Auditory / general approaches** | perception relies on **general** hearing mechanisms (not specific to speech) |
| **Exemplar models** | listeners store many specific **examples** and compare new input with them |

No theory is fully accepted; each explains some phenomena well. That is why this remains a lively field of research.

## The McGurk effect

McGurk and MacDonald (1976) showed viewers a video of a person saying /ga/ with the audio /ba/. Many people **hear /da/**, an "in-between" sound. This shows that the **visible mouth** takes part in hearing: speech perception is multisensory.

## Link with teaching

Understanding categorical perception helps explain why learners **cannot hear** a difference that their own language does not use to distinguish words (for example /l/ and /ɹ/ for Japanese speakers, or /ɪ/ and /iː/ for many learners). They hear through the **categories of their own language**.
