# Contrastive analysis and intelligibility

This lesson is for those who want to understand **why** Vietnamese speakers meet certain pronunciation difficulties, and **what to prioritise** when teaching.

## The contrastive analysis hypothesis

The **Contrastive Analysis Hypothesis** (**Lado 1957**) holds that **where the first language and the target language differ, learners will struggle**, because they **transfer** habits of their first language. Applied to Vietnamese speakers, based on earlier lessons, we *predict*:

| Difference | Prediction |
|---|---|
| No `/θ ð/` | replaced by `/t d/` or `/s z/` |
| No consonant clusters | dropping consonants or **inserting vowels** (*strengths*, *milk*) |
| Only a few final consonants | **loss** of final consonants, especially `/s z d/` (affecting grammar: -s, -ed) |
| Little use of vowel length | confusing `/ɪ/` and `/iː/` (ship/sheep) |
| Tones, no word stress | stressing syllables evenly, using pitch like a tone |
| Syllable-timed rhythm | little vowel reduction, few weak forms |
| `/tʰ/` and `/t/` contrast | hearing English (aspirated) `/t/` as `th` |

Such errors are described fairly often in teaching materials for Vietnamese learners of English, but should be regarded as **predictions** to be verified.

## Limits of contrastive analysis

- **Not every** difference causes errors, and sometimes **similarity** causes confusion.
- Some errors **do not come from the first language** but from the target language itself or the learning process (**developmental** errors).
- So **error analysis** (based on learners' real speech) and the notion of **interlanguage** (**Selinker 1972**) were added: the temporary system, with its own rules, that learners build between the two languages.

## Two goals: nativeness or intelligibility?

**Levis (2005)** distinguishes two principles:

- The **nativeness principle**: the goal is to sound **like a native speaker**.
- The **intelligibility principle**: the goal is that **listeners understand you**.

Today English is used mainly **between non-native speakers** (*English as a Lingua Franca*), so the second goal is increasingly supported.

## The "core" of intelligibility (Jenkins 2000)

**Jennifer Jenkins** studied conversations between non-native speakers and proposed a **phonological core** (the *Lingua Franca Core*) most important for mutual understanding:

| In the core (prioritise) | Outside the core (less important) |
|---|---|
| Most consonants | `/θ ð/` |
| Aspiration of `/p t k/` | Weak forms of function words |
| Keeping initial consonant clusters | Stress-timed rhythm |
| Vowel length (short/long) | Word stress |
| Nuclear stress | Detailed pitch movement |

This is still a **debated** topic (some researchers argue that dropping word stress or weak forms may harm intelligibility), but the key suggestion is **principled prioritisation** instead of correcting everything.

## Functional load

One more criterion: **functional load**, that is, **how many word pairs** a contrast distinguishes. The contrast `/p/-/b/` or `/l/-/ɹ/` has a high load; `/θ/-/ð/` is very low (almost no useful minimal pairs). Prioritising high-load contrasts is a practical argument.

## An attitude

A clear Vietnamese-accented English with reasonable stress is entirely a **good English accent**. The errors worth fixing are those that **reduce intelligibility**, not every trace of the first language.
