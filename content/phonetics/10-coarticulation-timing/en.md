# Coarticulation and timing

## Sounds are not "beads" on a string

When we transcribe `/kæt/` we get three separate symbols. But in speech their articulatory movements **overlap** in time. This is called **coarticulation**: the articulation of one sound is influenced by neighbouring sounds.

## Two directions of influence

- **Anticipatory** (right-to-left): a feature of a later sound appears early. For example, the lips begin rounding **before** reaching the vowel `/uː/` in *two* `/tuː/`, already during the `/t/`.
- **Carryover** (left-to-right): a feature of an earlier sound persists into a later one. For example, a vowel after a nasal consonant is slightly nasalised.

## Examples in English

| Example | What happens |
|---|---|
| *two* `/tuː/` vs *tea* `/tiː/` | the initial `/t/` has rounded lips in *two* but spread lips in *tea* |
| *man* | the middle vowel is nasalised by the neighbouring `/m/` and `/n/` |
| *key* vs *car* | `/k/` is pushed forward in *key* and back in *car*, following the next vowel |

## Targets and undershoot

A common model assumes each sound has an **articulatory target**. In fast speech the articulators **do not have time** to reach it, called **undershoot**: vowels are shortened and move towards the centre. This is one reason unstressed vowels drift towards `/ə/`.

## Coarticulation and assimilation

- **Coarticulation**: **mechanical, gradient** influence, present in every language.
- **Assimilation** in phonology: a sound **changes categorically** into another by rule. For example *in* + *possible* becomes *impossible*.

The boundary is blurry: assimilation can originate in coarticulation and over time become a stable rule.

## Articulation as "gestures"

One theoretical approach (*Articulatory Phonology*, Browman and Goldstein) takes the basic unit to be not the sound but the **articulatory gesture**: actions such as "close the lips", "raise the tongue tip", "open the glottis". Speech is the **temporal coordination** of these gestures; coarticulation is the natural result of gestures overlapping.

## Timing and VOT

**VOT** (voice onset time) is the clearest example of timing coordination: it measures the interval between the **release gesture** in the mouth and the **glottal closing gesture** for vibration. The `/p/`-`/b/` distinction in English is largely a timing difference, discussed in module 3.
