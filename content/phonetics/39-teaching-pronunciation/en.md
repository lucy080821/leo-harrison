# Teaching pronunciation with phonetics

This lesson is for **teachers**: using what you have learned in phonetics to teach pronunciation **on a sound basis**, not only with tips.

## A sensible goal

For most learners the realistic goal is **to be understood easily** and **to speak with confidence** (see the lesson on intelligibility), not to erase their first-language accent.

## Step 1: Diagnose

1. Collect a sample: **a short reading** and a few minutes of **free speech**.
2. Listen back and note errors in four groups: **individual sounds** (especially final consonants, clusters, vowel length), **word stress**, **rhythm and weak forms**, **intonation and sentence nucleus**.
3. Compare with the **predictions from Vietnamese** (the contrast lesson) to understand the **cause**.
4. If you have software, **measure** to have evidence (VOT, duration, formants, F0).

## Step 2: Set priorities

You can't fix everything at once. You can combine criteria:

- **Intelligibility** (Jenkins' core): consonants, aspiration, initial clusters, vowel length, nuclear stress.
- **Functional load**: prioritise contrasts that distinguish many word pairs (`/p b/`, `/l ɹ/`, `/ɪ iː/`).
- **Frequency** of the error and **grammatical impact** (loss of -s, -ed).
- **Fixability** and the learner's wishes.

## Step 3: A five-stage framework

**Celce-Murcia, Brinton and Goodwin (1996)** suggest:

1. **Description and analysis**: tell learners how the sound is heard and made (use the articulatory descriptions and figures from this course).
2. **Listening discrimination**: listen and choose the right sound or pair.
3. **Controlled practice**: read prepared word pairs and sentences.
4. **Guided practice**: prompted activities (gap fills, short dialogues).
5. **Communicative practice**: use the sound in free speech.

## Techniques

| Technique | Use for |
|---|---|
| **Minimal pairs** | listening and speaking confusable sounds |
| **Shadowing** (speaking right after a recording) | rhythm, stress, intonation |
| **Backchaining** | long words and sentences: *-ity, -bility, possibility* |
| **Gestures and visuals** | stress (clapping, a rubber band), tongue position (mirror, articulatory diagrams) |
| **Visual acoustic feedback** | seeing one's own spectrogram and F0 line |
| **Recording and listening back** | self-monitoring |

## Using the spectrogram as visual feedback

Learners often **cannot hear** a difference but can **see** it:

- **Aspiration**: put a sheet of paper in front of the mouth and say *pin* and *spin* (does it flutter or not). Then look at the **VOT** on the spectrogram.
- **Stress and rhythm**: compare the syllable durations and F0 line of the learner with a model.
- **Intonation**: look at the F0 line of a question and a statement.
- **Vowels**: compare the learner's F1-F2 with the standard chart (remember that women and children have **higher** formants than men).

## Feedback

- Be **selective**: one or two points at a time.
- **Praise first**, correct second, and give the learner a chance to try again.
- Don't interrupt while the learner is speaking freely; note it and respond afterwards.
- Distinguish **perception confusion** from **production difficulty**: the remedies differ.

## A 15-minute activity

Goal: **short and long** vowels, *ship* / *sheep*.

1. The teacher reads at random; learners raise a hand on hearing the long sound. (discrimination)
2. Show learners the **F1-F2** picture or the lip shapes: `/iː/` has spread lips and is longer. (description)
3. Repeat the word pair, then the sentence *The sheep is on the ship.* (controlled)
4. Learners make up a sentence with both words. (guided)
5. Pairs ask and answer freely about a trip to the sea. (communicative)

## An honest note

Research evidence on the effectiveness of each pronunciation technique is **not uniform**, and learners differ in goals, age and first language. So treat the suggestions above as a **starting point**, monitor your own learners' progress and adjust.
