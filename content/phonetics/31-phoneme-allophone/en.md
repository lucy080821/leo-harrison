# Phonemes, allophones and phonemic analysis

We described sounds at the phonetic level (modules 2 to 5). Now comes the question of **phonology**: which sounds does a language **treat as one**, and which as **different**?

## Phonemes and contrast

A **phoneme** is the smallest sound unit that **changes the meaning** of a word in a language. The main test is the **minimal pair**: two words differing in **one sound** in the same position.

- *pin* `/pɪn/` - *bin* `/bɪn/`: `/p/` and `/b/` are two English phonemes.
- Vietnamese *tai* `[tai]` - *thai* `[tʰai]`: `/t/` and `/tʰ/` are two Vietnamese phonemes.

A phoneme is an **abstract category**, realised by concrete sounds.

## Allophones

The concrete realisations of a phoneme are its **allophones**. They do not form minimal pairs with each other. There are two kinds of relation:

1. **Complementary distribution**: each allophone appears in its **own environment**, mutually exclusive.
   - English: `[pʰ]` at the start of a stressed syllable (*pin*) and `[p]` after /s/ (*spin*). No word contrasts through these two forms.
   - English: `[l]` before a vowel and `[ɫ]` after a vowel.
   - Spanish: `[b]` word-initially or after a nasal, `[β]` between vowels.
2. **Free variation**: two forms appear **in the same environment** without changing meaning; for example a word-final stop may be released or unreleased.

## Summary criteria

| Relation between two sounds | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| They form a minimal pair (contrast) | **two phonemes** |
| Complementary distribution, phonetically similar | **allophones of one phoneme** |
| Same environment, interchangeable without change of meaning | **free variants** |

Note the condition "**phonetically similar**": two sounds like `[h]` (syllable-initial) and `[ŋ]` (syllable-final) in English are in complementary distribution but too different to be counted as one phoneme.

## Steps of a phonemic analysis

1. Collect **data**: words containing the sounds in question, in narrow transcription.
2. Look for **minimal** or **near-minimal pairs**.
3. If none, examine the **environment** of each sound to find **complementary distribution**.
4. Check **phonetic similarity**.
5. Choose the **underlying phoneme** (usually the form with wider distribution or the more "natural" one) and write the **rule** (next lesson).

## Neutralisation

Sometimes a **contrast** is **lost** in a certain position. In German, *Rad* "wheel" and *Rat* "advice" are both pronounced `[ʁaːt]` in their base forms, because `/d/` and `/t/` **neutralise** word-finally (final devoicing). The two phonemes still differ elsewhere (*Rades* `[ʁaːdəs]` and *Rates* `[ʁaːtəs]`).

## Why this matters for learners

Each language "cuts" the sound space **in its own way**. A difference that is contrastive in the mother tongue is easy to hear, while a difference that is only allophonic is **ignored**. Vietnamese speakers hear `[tʰ]` and `[t]` very clearly because they contrast in Vietnamese, but to an English speaker they are the **same /t/**. Conversely the difference between `/θ/` and `/s/` is contrastive in English but absent in Vietnamese, so it is hard to hear (see modules 4 and 7).
