# Weak forms and connected speech

## Content words and function words

| Type | Includes | Usually |
|---|---|---|
| **Content words** | nouns, main verbs, adjectives, adverbs | **stressed** |
| **Function words** | articles, prepositions, pronouns, auxiliary verbs, conjunctions | usually **weak** |

For example: *I WANT to GO to the SHOP.* The capitalised words are stressed; the rest are **weak**.

## Weak forms

Function words have a **strong form** (when stressed, alone, or sentence-final) and a **weak form** (in normal speech). Weak forms often have a **vowel reduced** to `/ə/` or `/ɪ/` and sometimes **lose a consonant**:

| Word | Weak form | Strong form |
|---|---|---|
| the | `/ðə/` | `/ðiː/` |
| a | `/ə/` | `/eɪ/` |
| to | `/tə/` | `/tuː/` |
| for | `/fə/` | `/fɔː/` |
| and | `/ən/`, `/n̩/` | `/ænd/` |
| of | `/əv/` | `/ɒv/` |
| can | `/kən/` | `/kæn/` |
| was | `/wəz/` | `/wɒz/` |

The strong form appears when the word is **stressed** or **sentence-final**: *"Yes, I can."* `/kæn/`, *"Where are you from?"* `/frɒm/`.

## Connected speech

In fast speech, sounds at **word boundaries** influence each other. The main processes:

### 1. Linking

A final consonant attaches to the initial vowel of the next word: *pick it up* `/ˈpɪkɪtʌp/`.

**Linking /r/** (RP): /r/ is pronounced only when the next word starts with a vowel: *far away* `/ˌfɑːr əˈweɪ/`. Some speakers even insert an /r/ **not in the spelling** (*intrusive r*): *law and order* `/ˌlɔːr ən ˈɔːdə/`.

**Inserted glides**: between two vowels there is often `/j/` after `/iː aɪ eɪ/` (*see it* `/siː jɪt/`) and `/w/` after `/uː əʊ aʊ/` (*go on* `/ɡəʊ wɒn/`).

### 2. Elision

A sound is **dropped**, often `/t/` or `/d/` in the middle of a cluster: *next day* `/neks deɪ/`, *old man* `/əʊl mæn/`, *friendship* `/ˈfrenʃɪp/`.

### 3. Assimilation

A sound **changes** its place of articulation to resemble the following sound: *good boy* `/ɡʊb bɔɪ/`, *ten minutes* `/tem ˈmɪnɪts/`, *that person* `/ðæp ˈpɜːsn/`.

### 4. Coalescence

Two sounds **merge into one**: *did you* `/ˈdɪdʒu/`, *meet you* `/ˈmiːtʃu/`, *what you* `/ˈwɒtʃu/`.

## Style levels

These processes **vary with style**: common in casual, fast speech; less in formal, slow speech. They are not "errors" or "laziness" but ordinary rules of speech. For **listening**, knowing them helps you recognise "gonna", "wanna", "dijoo" as forms of familiar words.

## Meaning for teaching

Cruttenden and others stress that learners should **understand** these processes when listening, but **need not imitate all of them** when speaking. Speaking clearly word by word is entirely acceptable.
